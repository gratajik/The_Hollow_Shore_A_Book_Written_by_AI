# Writing and speaking in the documented Donald Trump voice

Read this profile before drafting or reviewing text against the documented voice.

This is a source-derived approximation based on public transcripts. Donald Trump did not
participate in, approve, or endorse it. It describes observable language patterns only. It
does not establish that source claims are true, supply positions for a new task, or make
generated text an authentic quotation.

Use only facts, positions, names, numbers, and commitments supplied for the current task.

## Authority order

When guidance conflicts, use this order:

1. Facts and constraints supplied for the current task
2. Register-matched evidence in this file
3. Descriptive rules
4. Provisional written adaptations

Samples outrank summary rules. Frequency matters: a pattern used occasionally in a source
becomes parody when inserted into every sentence.

## Voice in brief

The documented voice is direct, evaluative, personal, repetitive, and certainty-forward. It
usually reaches a verdict before explaining it. Long associative runs are broken by blunt
fragments, repeated phrases, rhetorical questions, rankings, numbers, and short declarations
of confidence. Claims often move through a personal anecdote or relationship before returning
to a simple conclusion. The intensity changes by setting: the rally register is the most
combative and audience-responsive; prepared addresses use longer policy blocks; press answers
are shorter, more improvisational, and more likely to begin with a clarification or hedge.

## Spoken versus written

### Spoken

Allow repetition, restarts, quick corrections, fragments, rhetorical questions, and
associative pivots. The speaker may interrupt a prepared line with a live observation, ask
the audience a question, or repeat a conclusion until it becomes the transition.

Do not clean every irregularity. The movement between polished material and spontaneous
comment is part of the documented rhythm.

### Written

Written guidance is inferred, not directly sampled. Start with a verdict. Keep paragraphs
short and centered on one claim. Preserve a limited amount of repetition, concrete ranking,
personal authority, and strong evaluation. Remove crowd responses, false starts, hearing
clarifications, and transcript artifacts.

Do not make the prose uniformly polished. One longer associative paragraph followed by a
short judgment is closer to the spoken evidence than a sequence of balanced, equally sized
paragraphs.

## Structure

### Openings

Open with thanks, recognition of the audience or place, and an immediate positive or
negative judgment. Establish relationship before policy detail.

### Development

A common sequence is:

1. State the verdict.
2. Intensify or repeat it.
3. Add a number, ranking, or personal anecdote.
4. Compare it with an opponent, earlier period, or worse alternative.
5. Return to the verdict in simpler language.

Longer remarks may leave this sequence for an anecdote and return several sentences later.

### Endings

End a section with a commitment, forecast, warning, or short judgment. Avoid a polished
aphorism. The accepted application test ended with:

> It's going to be a great thing for the city. People are going to use it, and they're going
> to love it.

campaign copy rather than the looser repetition and forecast found in the sampled speech.
